# Levinthal and Schliesmann (2025) — Cautious Exploitation

**Theory move.** The paper separates belief updating from search choice and shows why slow updating combined with exploitative choice can protect good alternatives against noisy negative feedback, with discovery-dominant tasks as a boundary. [Publisher HTML](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/orsc.2023.17538), *Organization Science* 36(2):903–917. **Coverage:** official HTML and user-supplied published PDF `4-pdf_download_AA2D74B7-97C5-4CF7-82E5-CA4E8CFEF4A5.pdf`, text/figure captions pp.903–917 checked; [earlier card](../orgsci_core_reading_pack/02_levinthal_schliesmann_2025.md), [source ledger](source_audit_first_five_2026_09_15.md). Online technical appendix not supplied.

1. **Phenomenon.** An organization must discover alternatives and evaluate whether the chosen one is truly good despite noisy outcomes [verified: introduction/discussion].
2. **Existing theory / assumption.** Reduced-form exploration rates can obscure whether switching follows deliberate experimentation or changed beliefs [verified: discussion].
3. **Why it matters.** The same observable switching can have distinct causes; treating action and cognition as one lever misstates the trade-off between discovery and persistence [analysis grounded in discussion].
4. **Mechanism.** Choice creates a binary payoff and information on the chosen alternative; a Bush–Mosteller fractional update changes only its attractiveness. Updating rate `φ` and exploration/choice parameter `τ` are varied independently [verified: Model §3.3]. Beliefs → choice → observed experience → beliefs; unchosen alternatives retain prior beliefs. The paper's alternatives are fixed while beliefs evolve, unlike our adaptive candidates.
5. **Design and non-obvious result.** Baseline 100 alternatives × 100 periods; `φ=.1–1` and `τ=0–.1` are crossed over 10,000 sampled opportunity environments. At noisy evaluation, `φ=.1,τ=0` loses about 21% relative to the optimal policy while `.1,.1` loses about 44%; the first policy searches less but persists with good alternatives. In deterministic pure-discovery settings, moderate explicit exploration regains value [PDF Results §§4.1–4.3, Figures 1–2,7].
6. **Contribution / limit.** Organizational adaptation is jointly generated by an information update and decision policy, not an exploration parameter alone. This is a stylized search model, not an estimate of hiring effects.
7. **Relation: test boundary.** Map feedback interpretation to candidate updating and skill-investment choice to action selection. **Hypothesis:** feedback noise × responsiveness could produce overreaction and evidence homogenization. A new factorial must hold signal precision separate from response speed and record investment targeting, skill diversity and later allocation; no such result is presently verified for our ABM.

## Systematic mechanism and evidence audit

**Theoretical counterfactual.** The paper does not merely ask whether exploration is good. Its pivotal separation is `belief updating (φ) × belief-to-action choice (τ)`: observed switching is not a primitive. Slow updating can preserve a favorable option through a single bad outcome, while accumulated bad outcomes can still cause a decisive switch under greedy choice. This opposes a simple equation of slow learning with behavioral inertia ([publisher, Introduction §§1–2 and Discussion §7](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/orsc.2023.17538)). The authors explicitly compare this partial-adjustment rule with the prior-experience-average belief rule; the latter's apparent need for moderate explicit exploration is partly an artifact of its inertia (publisher §2, near the transition to §3).

**Formal anatomy, paper-verified (publisher §§3.1–3.3).** One decision-making organization faces a fixed set of `N` alternatives. Each option has a latent Bernoulli success probability drawn from a Beta(2,2) distribution; the organization cannot see that probability, only the binary payoff of the chosen option. Baseline `N=100` and `T=100`; prior beliefs are equal to the distribution mean, 0.5. Softmax choice uses temperature `τ`: near zero, the best-believed option is chosen; larger values randomize choice. A Bush–Mosteller fractional adjustment with rate `φ∈[0,1]` changes the chosen option's belief after success or failure; *unchosen* beliefs remain fixed. Thus the endogenous stock is the belief vector, not option quality or an evolving population. The sequence is option choice → reward realization → chosen-belief update → next choice. No multi-agent synchronous-update issue arises; the precise pseudorandom stream or executable code is not supplied in this card.

**Design and unit, paper-verified (publisher §4.1, Figure 1).** Authors cross `φ=.1,.2,…,1` with `τ=0,.02,…,.10`, running each pair on 10,000 *drawn opportunity sets* of 100 alternatives. The opportunity set is held common across strategy comparisons; whether reward realizations are also paired is not stated here and must not be assumed. The unit for comparison is a sampled opportunity environment, with cumulative percent loss/regret against the optimal policy over 100 periods as the main outcome. `N=10`, extended horizons up to 10,000 and epsilon-greedy choice are explicit robustness/contrast settings (§§3.1–3.2, subsequent Results). This is not a ten-thousand-organization empirical dataset.

**Mediator audit, PDF-verified (Results §4.1, Figures 1–2, pp.909–911).** The authors distinguish *discovery* (quality of alternatives sampled/found) from *persistence* (staying with a good option despite noisy failures). At `φ=.1,τ=0`, the organization generally samples **7–8 of 100** options and finds an alternative around the **90th percentile**; greater `τ` can approach the **99th percentile**. The inferior-option persistence panel conditions on choosing an option *outside the top 10% in period 99* and asks whether it is repeated in period 100; slow-greedy search increases bad lock-in. The superior-option panel instead conditions on a *top-10% period-99* choice: `.1,0` persists approximately **100%** in the final period, while `.1,.1` persists about **4.8%**. These conditional denominators matter: the benefit is good-option persistence strong enough to offset weaker discovery and worse inferior-option trapping. The baseline regret comparison is roughly **21%** versus **44%** relative loss, not an absolute performance score.

## Published-PDF theory and design checks

**Exact belief rule (Model §§3.1–3.3, pp.907–908).** Each alternative has fixed latent Bernoulli success probability `p_i` sampled from Beta(2,2), but the organization observes only the binary payoff of the chosen arm. Beliefs `q_i` all start at 0.5. If the chosen option succeeds, `q_{i,t+1}=q_{i,t}+φ(1−q_{i,t})`; after failure, `q_{i,t+1}=(1−φ)q_{i,t}`; unchosen beliefs do not change. Softmax temperature `τ` maps the whole belief vector into action probabilities. At `τ=0`, the paper bypasses undefined softmax and selects the highest-believed option, randomly breaking ties (endnote 3, p.916). The organization can change action under greedy choice because a chosen option's belief can fall *below an unchosen prior* after accumulated failures. This is the minimum causal loop: `belief → action → stochastic outcome → chosen belief → next action`.

**Matched task environments but not assumed matched payoff streams (Results §4.1, p.909).** For each `(φ,τ)` pair, the paper faces 10,000 opportunity sets, each a fresh draw of `N` arm probabilities. The same sets are used across policy comparisons. Baseline `N=100,T=100`, parameter grid `φ=.1,.2,…,1; τ=0,.02,…,.1`. The independent comparison unit is the *drawn opportunity set* (with an organizational policy run on it); no appendix-provided seed log or explicit common Bernoulli stream for all policies is inspected. The paper's outcome is cumulative **percent loss versus choosing the best underlying arm each period**, which permits environment-normalized comparison (Figure 1). Do not recast it as candidate placement or an observed-data treatment effect.

**Belief-rule ablation/reconciliation (Results §4.2, Figures 3–5, pp.911–912).** Reducing arms to `N=10` does *not* move the best pair away from slow-greedy: relative loss is about **11%** there versus the 100-arm baseline's **21%**. The authors then replace constant fractional belief adjustment with a running average of experience. Under average updating, a moderate `τ` performs best (Figure 4); its *effective learning rate* drops rapidly with accumulated experience, from `.5` on a first new trial toward nearly zero by period 100 (Figure 5). Explicit exploration in that specification compensates for belief inertia rather than demonstrating a universal need to randomize choice. In the 10-arm comparison, constant slow-greedy yields about **11%** loss versus roughly **12%** with the best `τ=.04` under average updating (p.913). A statistically best estimator of arm value therefore need not induce the best adaptive *joint policy*.

**Turbulence: changed meaning of old evidence (Results §4.2, Figure 6, pp.913–914).** The paper varies shock probability `η=0–.32` for a 10-arm setting; after a shock, each arm may be redrawn from the original beta distribution with probability .5. Increasing turbulence makes the preferred `φ` faster because old trials become stale, while the preferred `τ` remains near greedy. At extreme `η=.32`, approximately **30%** relative loss for `φ=.3` versus **31%** for `φ=.1` demonstrates a *modest* optimization gain, not that fast updating suddenly dominates by a large margin. Turbulence here changes latent arm merits; it is not an evolving applicant population.

**Pure discovery: the decisive boundary isolation (Results §4.3, Figure 7, pp.914–915).** The authors remove evaluation noise by paying the underlying arm merit deterministically on each trial, while leaving 100 arms and the `φ×τ` grid. One sample now reveals true merit; under `τ=0`, choice behaves like a stopping rule at the first option above the untried prior. When `φ` is optimized for each `τ`, a *moderate* explicit exploration rate performs best (Figure 7b), whereas very high `τ` oversearches. This directly distinguishes a scarcity-of-discovery problem from a noisy-evaluation/persistence problem. It is stronger mechanism isolation than simply changing the number of arms.

**Robustness and limits (endnotes, pp.916–917).** The main text reports qualitative robustness to Beta(2,10) through Beta(10,2) opportunity structures, epsilon-greedy choice, and extended horizons up to 10,000 periods. On a very long horizon the best `φ` moves slightly from `.1` to `.2` while the preferred explicit search remains greedy. Technical-appendix cells and software reproducibility are not separately audited. Most importantly, option merits are fixed except in the turbulence counterfactual; in our recruitment theory candidates invest in latent skills and evidence, so the option set becomes endogenous. That is an assumption change requiring new model evidence, not a prediction imported from this paper.

**Boundary as a changed assumption.** If a promising option's merit is immediately apparent after discovery, evaluation noise ceases to be the principal problem: explicit exploration has a stronger role. Where repeated noisy trials are required to estimate merit, slow updating filters noise. A long horizon can also change the best learning rate because initially useful persistence eventually competes with learning enough to distinguish alternatives (publisher Introduction and later sensitivity analyses). The paper's test is about *evaluation quality versus discovery scarcity*, not a universal recommendation for low `φ`.

**Transfer and falsifier (our analysis, not run).** In recruitment, employer evaluation can change candidates' beliefs about which skills to invest in, and the candidate's investment policy can be more or less greedy conditional on those beliefs. A minimal factorial would independently vary feedback reliability and belief-update speed while keeping skill-choice stochasticity separate. Record belief changes, number of skills tried, persistence in beneficial investment, evidence concentration and next-period matching. If slower belief updating does not reduce noise-driven abandonment under unreliable feedback—or if its advantage remains unchanged when evaluation becomes transparent—the proposed analogy fails. Fixed-option bandit results do **not** establish that recruitment AI improves talent quality, because candidate skill stocks and the option set become endogenous in our theory.
