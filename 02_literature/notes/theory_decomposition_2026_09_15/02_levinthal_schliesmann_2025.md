# Levinthal and Schliesmann (2025) — Cautious Exploitation

**Theory move.** The paper separates belief updating from search choice and shows why slow updating combined with exploitative choice can protect good alternatives against noisy negative feedback, with discovery-dominant tasks as a boundary. [Publisher HTML](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/orsc.2023.17538), *Organization Science* 36(2):903–917. **Coverage:** substantial publisher HTML; [earlier card](../orgsci_core_reading_pack/02_levinthal_schliesmann_2025.md). Exact figures and numerical values quoted in the user's shared chat remain pending independent locator checks.

1. **Phenomenon.** An organization must discover alternatives and evaluate whether the chosen one is truly good despite noisy outcomes [verified: introduction/discussion].
2. **Existing theory / assumption.** Reduced-form exploration rates can obscure whether switching follows deliberate experimentation or changed beliefs [verified: discussion].
3. **Why it matters.** The same observable switching can have distinct causes; treating action and cognition as one lever misstates the trade-off between discovery and persistence [analysis grounded in discussion].
4. **Mechanism.** Choice creates a binary payoff and information on the chosen alternative; a Bush–Mosteller fractional update changes only its attractiveness. Updating rate `φ` and exploration/choice parameter `τ` are varied independently [verified: Model §3.3]. Beliefs → choice → observed experience → beliefs; unchosen alternatives retain prior beliefs. The paper's alternatives are fixed while beliefs evolve, unlike our adaptive candidates.
5. **Design and non-obvious result.** The official text describes a baseline with 100 alternatives and 100 periods, comparative learning/search strategies, and cumulative loss against an optimal policy. Slow updating plus exploitative choice performs well where evaluation is noisy, even though deliberate discovery can suffer; explicit exploration gains value where discovery predominates. Environmental change shifts the desirable updating speed [verified: Model/Results/Discussion]. Complete replication and every numeric contrast require figure-level audit.
6. **Contribution / limit.** Organizational adaptation is jointly generated by an information update and decision policy, not an exploration parameter alone. This is a stylized search model, not an estimate of hiring effects.
7. **Relation: test boundary.** Map feedback interpretation to candidate updating and skill-investment choice to action selection. **Hypothesis:** feedback noise × responsiveness could produce overreaction and evidence homogenization. A new factorial must hold signal precision separate from response speed and record investment targeting, skill diversity and later allocation; no such result is presently verified for our ABM.

## Systematic mechanism and evidence audit

**Theoretical counterfactual.** The paper does not merely ask whether exploration is good. Its pivotal separation is `belief updating (φ) × belief-to-action choice (τ)`: observed switching is not a primitive. Slow updating can preserve a favorable option through a single bad outcome, while accumulated bad outcomes can still cause a decisive switch under greedy choice. This opposes a simple equation of slow learning with behavioral inertia ([publisher, Introduction §§1–2 and Discussion §7](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/orsc.2023.17538)). The authors explicitly compare this partial-adjustment rule with the prior-experience-average belief rule; the latter's apparent need for moderate explicit exploration is partly an artifact of its inertia (publisher §2, near the transition to §3).

**Formal anatomy, paper-verified (publisher §§3.1–3.3).** One decision-making organization faces a fixed set of `N` alternatives. Each option has a latent Bernoulli success probability drawn from a Beta(2,2) distribution; the organization cannot see that probability, only the binary payoff of the chosen option. Baseline `N=100` and `T=100`; prior beliefs are equal to the distribution mean, 0.5. Softmax choice uses temperature `τ`: near zero, the best-believed option is chosen; larger values randomize choice. A Bush–Mosteller fractional adjustment with rate `φ∈[0,1]` changes the chosen option's belief after success or failure; *unchosen* beliefs remain fixed. Thus the endogenous stock is the belief vector, not option quality or an evolving population. The sequence is option choice → reward realization → chosen-belief update → next choice. No multi-agent synchronous-update issue arises; the precise pseudorandom stream or executable code is not supplied in this card.

**Design and unit, paper-verified (publisher §4.1, Figure 1).** Authors cross `φ=.1,.2,…,1` with `τ=0,.02,…,.10`, running each pair on 10,000 *drawn opportunity sets* of 100 alternatives. The opportunity set is held common across strategy comparisons; whether reward realizations are also paired is not stated here and must not be assumed. The unit for comparison is a sampled opportunity environment, with cumulative percent loss/regret against the optimal policy over 100 periods as the main outcome. `N=10`, extended horizons up to 10,000 and epsilon-greedy choice are explicit robustness/contrast settings (§§3.1–3.2, subsequent Results). This is not a ten-thousand-organization empirical dataset.

**Mediator audit.** The authors distinguish *discovery* (quality of alternatives sampled/found) from *persistence* (staying with a good option despite noisy failures). Their analysis reports that a very slow greedy strategy samples few of the 100 options and does not discover the absolute best option reliably, yet obtains a strong persistence benefit; greater explicit randomization improves discovery but can abandon a promising choice. This makes final regret an interaction of two opposing channels, not a one-parameter monotone curve (publisher §4, discovery/persistence analysis). The precise percentile and round-by-round values require figure-level extraction before quoting them in a manuscript.

**Boundary as a changed assumption.** If a promising option's merit is immediately apparent after discovery, evaluation noise ceases to be the principal problem: explicit exploration has a stronger role. Where repeated noisy trials are required to estimate merit, slow updating filters noise. A long horizon can also change the best learning rate because initially useful persistence eventually competes with learning enough to distinguish alternatives (publisher Introduction and later sensitivity analyses). The paper's test is about *evaluation quality versus discovery scarcity*, not a universal recommendation for low `φ`.

**Transfer and falsifier (our analysis, not run).** In recruitment, employer evaluation can change candidates' beliefs about which skills to invest in, and the candidate's investment policy can be more or less greedy conditional on those beliefs. A minimal factorial would independently vary feedback reliability and belief-update speed while keeping skill-choice stochasticity separate. Record belief changes, number of skills tried, persistence in beneficial investment, evidence concentration and next-period matching. If slower belief updating does not reduce noise-driven abandonment under unreliable feedback—or if its advantage remains unchanged when evaluation becomes transparent—the proposed analogy fails. Fixed-option bandit results do **not** establish that recruitment AI improves talent quality, because candidate skill stocks and the option set become endogenous in our theory.
