# Kane and Alavi (2007) — Information Technology and Organizational Learning

**Theory move.** The paper extends March's learning model with distinct IT-enabled learning mechanisms and shows their exploration/exploitation effects depend on context and combinations. [Publisher record](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/orsc.1070.0286), *Organization Science* 18(5):796–812. **Coverage:** user-supplied JSTOR published PDF `5-25146139.pdf`, printed pp.796–812 including Table 1, Table 3, Figures 1–6 and Appendices A–B; [source ledger](source_audit_first_five_2026_09_15.md). The user's detailed explanation served as a question list; claims below are now checked against original text unless labeled `our analysis` or `not assessable`.

1. **Phenomenon.** IT supports learning, but different tools need not create the same exploration/exploitation trajectory [verified: abstract].
2. **Existing theory / assumption.** March's model provides a baseline without these tool-specific channels. Treating IT as a single intervention hides differences [first claim verified in abstract; latter is our interpretation].
3. **Why it matters.** If mechanisms interact with structure and environment, a generic adoption effect is theoretically under-specified [analysis].
4. **Mechanism.** Prior Company Z case evidence and 20 semistructured interviews map KRP, TR and ECOP into three distinct *learning-source* algorithms on an extended March model [Table 1 pp.799–800, Endnote p.811, Model pp.801–803]. Exact rule inventory follows.
5. **Design / non-obvious result.** Thirty simulations per parameter set compare pure/augmented/blended tools, knowledge/variance time paths, individual learning rate, turnover and turbulence [Table 3 p.804, Figures 1–6 pp.805–808]. Short-run winners, long-run winners and contingency rankings differ.
6. **Contribution / limit.** Tool affordances should be theorized by what learning channel they alter; the simulation does not establish causal effects of real-world IT adoption.
7. **Relation: adopt channel decomposition.** R1 ranking, R2 explanation and R3 active tasks can be distinguished as evaluation, guidance and intervention. This is our mapping, not their finding. **Test:** separate channel toggles and neutral controls before claiming a particular mechanism; sequential regime contrasts may bundle more than one difference.

## Theoretical question and predecessor

The publisher abstract and original text verify that the authors use Company Z case evidence to extend March (1991) with communication technology, knowledge repositories and groupware. The sharper theoretical reconstruction is: IT should be decomposed by *who learns from whom*, persistence of shared knowledge and whether members see a common or differentiated source. March lets individuals learn from the code and the code learn from superior individuals, but **not** individuals directly from each other (March p.75; Kane p.799). Kane explicitly adds direct interpersonal learning and a project-team partition before introducing the three tool architectures (pp.799–801). The consequential assumption change is therefore the *information path*, not software adoption as a binary treatment. The paper also warns that quantitative March-versus-extended-model comparisons are difficult because baseline extensions change dynamics (Discussion p.809).

## Mechanism architecture: working rule inventory

| Channel | Information path in the user's explanation | Predicted intermediate stock | Evidence status |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `KRP` repository/portal | expert majority vetting → durable team repositories → portal search across higher-knowledge repositories | Common, persistent knowledge; rapid homogenization | Primary-verified Table 1 p.800; Model pp.801–802 |
| `TR` team room | higher-knowledge same-team member → team learner | Within-team convergence, between-team differences | Primary-verified Model pp.801–802; Figure 2 analysis p.804 |
| `ECOP` electronic community of practice | interest-network peers, occasional outside-interest links → partial-dimension communication | Differentiated sources and slower convergence | Primary-verified Table 1 p.800; Model pp.802–803; Table 3 p.804 |

The distinctions `global common / local common / distributed heterogeneous` remain **our analytical compression** of original rules, not author terminology. The authors explicitly use *closed versus open knowledge transformation cycles* to interpret the result (p.806). Their verified mechanism chain is `IT architecture → heterogeneous or common knowledge sources/recipients → knowledge variance and accessibility → short/long-run learning`; the “access” condition matters because TR preserves between-team variation but cannot deliver it to those who would benefit (p.804).

## Experiments: conditional pattern reported in the supplied explanation

Original Results p.804 and Figures 1–2 p.805 verify that pure `KRP`/`TR` improve knowledge quickly then plateau, while `ECOP` overtakes them around **Round 15**. Figure 2 and p.804 identify the mediator: KRP variance drops sharply in the first two rounds and is nearly absent by Round 5; ECOP initially raises it and still shows `K_var=.02` at Round 80 (the latter is **text-reported, not plotted**); TR retains mainly *between-team* variation. Variance alone is insufficient because the relevant knowledge must reach the person able to use it. This is primary-text verified as a modeled mechanism, not an empirical estimate of organizations adopting those tools.

The paper implements pure (`100/0/0`), augmented (`80/10/10`, `60/20/20`) and blended (`33/33/33`) tool-use mixes by giving each individual a fresh probabilistic tool choice **each round**; the prior round's choice does not determine the next (Model p.803; Table 3 p.804). Figures 1–3 and the p.806 interpretation verify that external input helps a `TR` core escape its plateau, KRP-dominant mixtures show little additional gain *except the equally blended condition needs its own qualification*, and homogenizing tools erode ECOP-dominant advantages—marginally for population knowledge in Figure 1 but more strongly for code knowledge at Round 80 in Figure 3. Therefore the substantive result is an architecture interaction, not an additive `A+B` benefit. The exact visual ordinates of plotted curves should not be invented from OCR.

Figure 4 p.807 verifies that increasing `p1` helps ECOP far more than KRP/TR: network structure already supplies exploration, so slow individual learning under ECOP can yield too much exploration and weak exploitation. Figure 5 pp.807–808 verifies that ECOP is most vulnerable to turnover, whereas TR is most resistant at high tested turnover; the authors explain newcomer assimilation through common or local codified sources versus network-based reconstruction. Figure 6 pp.808–809 verifies that at `p4=.01/.03`, ECOP better resists degrading knowledge under turbulence by retaining divergent beliefs from which newly useful superior individuals can emerge. These are conditional simulation findings, not proof about real-world IT installations. Table 3 p.804 confirms `p1={.1,.5,.9}`, turnover `{0,.1,.3}`, turbulence `{0,.01,.03}`, ECOP richness `{.2,.33}` and additional sensitivity ranges; Results p.803 says **30 runs per parameter set**, and Appendix B p.811 says belief changes go into `t+1` arrays before simultaneous end-of-round update.

## What a computational owner should learn and test

The paper's strongest methodological lesson, **as verified at abstract level**, is case evidence → distinct technology mechanisms → extended March model → combination and environment comparisons. The user's reconstruction adds a useful audit checklist: state, source network, decision/update rule, mediator, short versus long outcome, combinations, turnover and turbulence, sensitivity, and update-order artifact. For our ABM, write a corresponding table for `R1` ranking, `R2` explanations and `R3` tasks: exactly which observable information reaches which candidate, when, and whether it changes latent skill or only evidence. A possible new test is feedback-source diversity × candidate response speed, keeping feedback accuracy fixed and logging investment concentration. A falsifier is no interaction or no mediator change after source diversity is varied. **This is an experiment proposal, not an archived simulation result.**

## Twenty-two-check primary-text audit for reuse

1. **Surface versus real question.** Surface: which IT supports organizational learning? Theory: which information-source/recipient architecture preserves variance or accelerates knowledge convergence under a particular environment (Introduction pp.796–798; Results/Discussion pp.804–810). A tool name is not a treatment mechanism.
2. **Predecessor and changed assumption.** March's individual↔code loop has no direct individual↔individual transmission. Kane adds interpersonal learning, project teams, then three source-selection architectures (pp.799–801). Its explicit design is an *extension*, not a claim March had no useful organizational learning theory.
3. **Empirical observation → formal rule.** Table 1 p.800 joins Company Z observations, case quotations and model specifications. The endnote p.811 reports **20 semistructured telephone interviews** lasting 45–75 minutes, not 20 organizations or a statistically representative sample. KRP vetting, password-protected team rooms and interest-based ECOPs are grounded by those observations but the exact probabilities are model choices subjected to sensitivity analysis.
4. **Actors, levels and stocks.** Reality has 30 independent `±1` dimensions; the code and 100 individual knowledge vectors use `{−1,0,+1}` (Appendix A p.810). Each person's knowledge level is the *share of dimensions matching reality*, while agents do not observe the truth of each dimension; they are assumed able to identify globally more knowledgeable sources (Appendix A p.811). Ten fixed project teams of ten members provide a local level (Model p.801); team repositories persist across periods (p.802). Reality changes only in turbulence scenarios.
5. **Common learning rule; controlled source count.** All tools form a focal individual's `Φ` group of sources with **higher overall knowledge level** and adopt that group's dimension-wise majority using the same individual-learning probability `p1` (Model pp.800–803). Authors say tool parameters were chosen to make the *number of available learning sources approximately comparable*, narrowing the interpretation toward architectural differences rather than more sources per se (p.801). That equalization is a design aim, not a fully matched network topology.
6. **KRP micro-rule.** Above-team-average knowledgeable members act as subject matter experts. A contribution passes vetting only for dimensions matching their majority. Each team repository stores accepted content for the simulation's duration, aggregates old and new stored knowledge into a majority vector at the start of a round, and the portal lets an individual learn from repositories whose overall knowledge is higher than the individual's (Table 1 p.800; Model pp.801–802). `Filter→aggregate→retain→diffuse` is our compression. Stale knowledge is intentionally **not deleted**, based on Company Z observation.
7. **TR micro-rule.** The eligible superior `Φ_TR` sources are *same-project-team individuals*, and the focal learner probabilistically adopts the majority on each dimension (p.802). Team membership remains fixed in the baseline (p.801). It is locally closed, but the outcome's between-team variation means it is not a globally homogenizing repository.
8. **ECOP micro-rule.** Members are randomly placed into four interest groups; each assembles a subnetwork from same-interest members with probability `p_i=.25` and a small number of other-interest members with `p_ni=.02`. The *source eligibility/knowledge level* uses whole-vector merit, but communication exposes only a random `R_ct` share of dimensions before `p1` adoption (pp.802–803). Baseline reported `R_ct=.2/.33`; `p_i=.15/.35`, `p_ni=.01/.03` and core–periphery networks are sensitivity checks (Table 3 p.804). Leanness changes transmitted content, not reputation screening.
9. **Within-period sequence/update-order audit.** Appendix B p.811 lays out initialize → ECOP networks → current knowledge levels → each individual's tool choice and superior group → dimension-wise individual learning → code learning from above-code individuals → turnover/network reset and environmental dimension flips → output/end-round promotion of `t+1` arrays. All knowledge changes are first stored and *simultaneously* applied at round end, preventing earlier-updated persons from becoming later persons' learning sources in the same round. The published flowchart, not supplied executable code, supports this conclusion.
10. **Parameters and horizon.** Table 3 p.804 distinguishes *final model* from additional sensitivity values: `p2=1` in final tool analyses despite lower p2 sensitivity checks; `p1=.1/.5/.9`; `p3=0/.1/.3`; `p4=0/.01/.03`; ten teams; `R_ct=.2/.33`; mixed-tool shares as above. Short-term means Rounds **1–30**, long-term comparison Round **80** (p.804). Do not mistake Round 80 for all trajectories' plotted horizon.
11. **Experimental unit and repetition.** Results p.803 states **30 stochastic executions per parameter specification**. Thus the simulation run is the unit; the PDF does not document a modern paired-seed policy, seed identifiers, common-random-number design or software release. A plot of mean knowledge must not be read as 30 independent companies observed in the field.
12. **Outcome/mediator definitions.** The authors inspect average population knowledge `K_pop`, organizational-code knowledge `K_code`, and population knowledge variance `K_var` (pp.803–804; Figures 1–3). `K_var` is an intermediate diversity indicator, not performance by itself; TR shows why access to variation is also necessary (p.804). Appendix A defines knowledge against reality, although the agents cannot identify which dimension is correct.
13. **Single-tool short/long contrast.** Pure KRP/TR rise rapidly and plateau, whereas ECOP surpasses them around Round 15 and continues improving in the first-30-round trajectory (p.804; Figure 1 p.805). KRP nearly exhausts variance by Round 5; ECOP's `K_var=.02` at Round 80 is text reported but not graphed; TR retains mostly cross-team variation (p.804; Figure 2 p.805). These are contingent on the displayed `p1=.5,R_ct=.33` configuration.
14. **Combination interaction, not “all IT helps.”** Fresh per-round tool draws give pure, augmented and blended shares (p.803; Table 3). TR-dominant mixtures import external knowledge and escape local plateau; KRP-dominant mixes mostly maintain global convergence; ECOP-dominant mixes lose some of its longer-run advantage, especially in `K_code` (Figures 1–3 pp.805–806). Figure 3 uses `p1=.9,R_ct=.2` at Round 80, so it is not simply the endpoint of Figure 1's `p1=.5,R_ct=.33` trajectories.
15. **Mechanism post-analysis.** The p.806 open/closed knowledge-transformation-cycle account interprets the figures: shared sources/recipients create repeated homogenizing feedback; differentiated sources/recipients sustain personalized recombination. The *observed in-model* evidence is variance and knowledge trajectories; the closed/open conceptual language is the authors' interpretation. The cycle itself is not separately randomized as a treatment.
16. **Learning-rate interaction.** Figure 4 p.807 compares `p1=.1,.5,.9` at Round 80 (`R_ct=.2`). KRP/TR vary less; ECOP improves strongly with fast individual uptake because its *structure* supplies exploratory difference. Slow ECOP learning can mean too much exploration and insufficient exploitation (text p.807). This qualifies March's slow-learner prescription rather than contradicting March's model on identical assumptions.
17. **Turnover boundary.** Figure 5 pp.807–808 (`p1=.9,R_ct=.2`, Round 80) shows ECOP dropping fastest when `p3` rises; by `p3=.2` it is below the other two, while TR is most resistant at high tested turnover. Table 3's final result grid lists `p3=0,.1,.3`; `.2` occurs in Figure/text as an intermediate reference, not a listed primary grid value. The paper's assimilation/knowledge-loss explanation is an author mechanism argument, not a directly plotted newcomer-learning-speed series.
18. **Turbulence boundary.** Figure 6 pp.808–809 displays first-30-round paths at `p4=0,.01,.03`, `p1=.9,R_ct=.2`. Exploitative tools plateau/degrade under change; ECOP preserves alternate beliefs so some individuals become newly superior after reality shifts. This is the opposite contingency from high turnover, showing fit between architecture and environmental condition rather than a universally best tool.
19. **Sensitivity and scope.** Table 3 p.804 checks additional rates, richness, team count (`2,5,20,50`), and ECOP topology. Authors say noninfluential sensitivity variables were set aside in detailed Results (p.803). Do not convert that screening into a claim that *every* arbitrary network or distribution was structurally robust.
20. **Author-stated limitations.** Knowledge is only 30 discrete dimensions, actors are idealized as identifying globally superior sources, and tool mappings come from one organization whose technologies may be used differently elsewhere (Limitations p.810). The authors explicitly note low individual learning and high turnover as conditions where ECOP is not superior. These qualifications are part of the theory, not a defensive afterthought.
21. **Recruitment transfer (our analysis; untested).** For R1 ranking, R2 explanations and R3 tasks, write the information route, source of guidance, recipient eligibility, persistence of evidence, uptake rule and later feedback source separately. A minimal test is `common broadcast versus differentiated guidance × feedback reliability`, measuring accessibility to useful advice, investment targeting, skill/evidence variance and later matching. A no-mediator-change result would falsify our channel analogy. This paper does **not** show that a hiring AI changes applicants' skill investment.
22. **Manuscript/computation handoff.** The paper can support a theory claim that organizational technology mechanisms interact through learning architecture and environmental fit; it cannot support any numerical AI recruitment prediction. New experiments belong in a versioned config/script under computation ownership, with the locked baseline and paired-seed policy preserved; no such new experiment was run here.

**Audit outcome.** The supplied original verifies most of the user's Kane explanation's substantive mechanism chain. Important precision gains are the per-round *fresh tool choice*, `p2=1` final model, Figure 1 versus Figure 3's different `p1/R_ct` conditions, TR's retained but inaccessible between-team variance, and the distinction between text-reported Round-80 variance and plotted short-run curves. Remaining uncertainty is the unreleased executable implementation/random-stream policy, not the main-text rule or figure existence. The PDF has not been redistributed via Git.
