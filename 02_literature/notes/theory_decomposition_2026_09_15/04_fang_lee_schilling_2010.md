# Fang, Lee and Schilling (2010) — Isolation of Subgroups and Organizational Learning

**Theory move.** Fang, Lee and Schilling relax March's code-mediated-only learning architecture by allowing direct interpersonal learning on a tunable subgroup network, showing that a small but nonzero level of intergroup linking can preserve diversity *and* diffuse useful knowledge when the search problem is moderately hard. [Publisher record](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/orsc.1090.0468), *Organization Science* 21(3):625–642. **Coverage:** user-supplied 2008 [SSRN author working paper](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=960273), 49 pp., including Model, Results, Figures 1–8, Tables 1–3 and Appendices A–C; **not yet cross-checked against the 2010 final publication**. See [source ledger](source_audit_first_five_2026_09_15.md).

1. **Phenomenon.** Organizational learning benefits from sharing useful knowledge but may converge too quickly [verified: author-draft Introduction and Results].
2. **Existing theory / assumption.** More communication need not improve learning monotonically; treating diffusion separately from preservation of heterogeneity obscures a tension [our reconstruction from the author-draft Introduction and Model].
3. **Why it matters.** Dense connections transmit good ideas and can remove independent exploration; isolation retains variation but prevents diffusion [analysis].
4. **Mechanism.** A rewired connected-caveman graph (`β`) defines who can learn from whom. Each member compares *overall payoff* to directly connected peers, forms a group of superior neighbors, and probabilistically adopts its dimension-wise majority belief; updates use the same start-of-period state (author draft Model §§3–3.3, pp.13–18).
5. **Design / result.** Baseline `n=280, m=100, z=7, s=5, p_learning=.3`; 200 stochastic runs per `β` for equilibrium curves. The author draft reports a broad peak around `β=.05–.1`, faster initial learning but lower equilibrium quality as linking rises beyond that interval, and robustness/limits under subgroup size, complexity, turnover and episodic reality changes (author draft Results §§4.1–4.3, Figures 2–8).
6. **Contribution / limit.** Structure can balance diffusion and diversity in the simulated organization; there is no direct result about common AI guidance or applicants.
7. **Relation: boundary hypothesis.** Standardized feedback could act as an institutional broadcast that aligns investments while narrowing skill variety. This analogy is **inferred and untested**; distinguish signal accuracy from broadcast reach, then measure short-run alignment, diversity and recovery after demand changes.

## Primary-text theory-to-rule and evidence map (2008 author draft)

**Actual theory question and predecessor.** How much cross-group communication allows superior knowledge to diffuse *without* destroying the independently developed belief sets that make later learning possible? The draft compares March's individual↔code socialization with direct individual↔individual learning, then joins Wright-style population subdivision and small-world network ideas to make the information architecture manipulable (Theory §2 and Model §3, pp.6–15). This is not “more connections are good” or “silos are good.” Both pure isolation and indiscriminate mixing omit one necessary path.

**Formal anatomy (draft Model §§3.1–3.3, pp.13–18).** Reality is an exogenous `m`-component `±1` vector; every individual holds `m` beliefs in `{−1,0,+1}`. The organization is a graph of individuals, not a separately adapting March code. A modified Watts connected-caveman network starts with clustered subgroups and minimal bridges, so `β=0` is *nearly isolated but still connected*, **not complete isolation**. Each existing link is randomly rewired to an out-of-cluster contact with probability `β`; small `β` adds shortcuts while preserving subgroup identity, `β→1` destroys it. Edges are bidirectional learning routes. Agents see their own and directly linked peers' *whole-string payoff*, but cannot see which belief dimension caused that payoff. A focal member filters directly connected peers with higher total payoff, takes the majority belief separately on each dimension, and adopts it with probability `p_learning`; without superior peers or with a ±1 tie, the old belief remains. All members learn simultaneously from beliefs at the beginning of the period. Learning ends at no further individual belief change in the stable baseline. Thus `β` alters the *source set* while `p_learning` alters the *uptake rule*; these must not be confused.

**Search difficulty is a second controlled assumption, not a network effect (Model §3.2; Appendix A, pp.16–17,48).** `s` partitions `m` dimensions into independent bundles of `s`, with payoff for a bundle only if its coupled dimensions are correct. At `s=1`, the payoff reduces to March's additive number of correct beliefs; at `s=m`, only an exact complete solution earns payoff. Baseline `s=5` and `m=100` produce a moderately difficult problem. Organizational performance is the *mean member payoff*, not the payoff of a code or probability of hiring.

**Baseline and experimental unit (Model §3.3; Results §4.1, pp.18–22).** The baseline uses `n=280` members, `40` clusters of `z=7`, `m=100`, `s=5`, `p_learning=.3`. Initial belief and reality vectors are random. Every `β` value is run 200 times for the equilibrium comparison in Figure 3, which displays mean and 10th/90th percentile outcomes; Figure 2 is a typical trajectory, not the uncertainty estimate. The independent unit is a stochastic *organization run*. The draft does not report that the same initialized organization, rewiring random stream or payoff randomness is paired across all `β`; do not call this a paired-seed design. The stop-at-equilibrium rule applies to the stable baseline; the turbulent extension has periodic shocks and is evaluated over its own time path.

**Short/long reversal and mediator (Figures 2–4, pp.20–22).** More `β` generally improves *initial learning speed*, but beyond the small-link region it lowers equilibrium mean payoff. The draft locates the peak at roughly `β∈[.05,.1]`; `β=0` yields both slow/low mean and a much wider 10th–90th percentile spread (Figure 3). To test why, it defines a pairwise belief-dissimilarity index over all `n(n−1)/2` individual pairs and `m` dimensions (Eq.1, p.21; Figure 4). Large `β` drains variation quickly; very low `β` preserves variation but blocks effective access to superior outsiders. This supports a *two-mediator* causal account: retained heterogeneity × cross-group accessibility. The text's completely disconnected and fully connected tests are explicitly **unreported numerical analyses**, not Figure 3 treatments; do not merge them with `β=0/1`.

**Micro-mechanism of the nonlinear effect (Results “How Learning Unfolds,” pp.24–25).** Within clusters, overlapping contact neighborhoods and the superior-peer majority rule produce common local belief sets. Different clusters can stabilize on different locally improved sets. A superior outside contact often fails to alter the focal agent if several superior inside contacts dominate the majority; after the focal/inside group improves, outside ideas can enter and recombine. This delays premature global imitation without sealing groups. At very high rewiring, early superior strings can travel across the whole graph before independent cluster-level recombination occurs. This is the exact organizational information path to borrow, rather than a vague diversity claim.

**Mechanism isolation, draft Appendix C (p.49).** Replacing majority-of-superior-neighbors with *tournament selection*—copy the single best linked neighbor—makes subgroup structure matter much less. Tournament copying does better than majority at very low or high `β`, but worse in the semi-isolated region in the displayed appendix curve. This is an important boundary: a network architecture effect depends on how people interpret and combine available source information. It is an author-draft test, not proof that all alternative learning rules would erase the result.

**Subgroup-size and problem boundaries (Figure 5/Table 1, pp.23,47; Figure 6, pp.26–28).** Increasing `z` while keeping the total population comparable depresses performance and largely retains the β inverted-U. However, `z` simultaneously changes the average number of neighbors (e.g., 6 at `z=7`, 13 at `z=14`), so this is not a clean “group size only” experiment; the authors discuss this confounding in note 14. At `m=100`, very easy bundles (`s=1/2`) let all architectures approach maximum payoff; excessively interdependent bundles (around `s≥10` in that panel) give all architectures near-zero learning. The semi-isolated advantage appears for *intermediate* difficulty. Authors caution that an exact numerical `s` boundary depends on `m` and divisibility; do not transplant `2<s<10` as a universal threshold.

**Turnover × episodic environmental change (Figures 7–8, Tables 2–3, pp.28–30,47).** Each member exits independently with probability `p_turnover` and is replaced by a random-belief newcomer. Reality does **not** flip each dimension every round as in March/Kane: every `T=200` periods, each reality dimension is flipped with probability `p_envir` (draft p.28). At β=.1, turnover supplies variation and permits recovery after shocks; without it the system loses recovery ability (Figure 7). The authors compare β=0,.1,1 over 100 organization runs per condition and `p_turnover={0,.005,.01,.05,.1,.5,1}` with `p_envir={.1,.4,.7}` (p.29). Table 2 reports, at `p_turnover=.01, p_envir=.1`, mean performance `44.45` (β=0), `61.49` (β=.1), `54.47` (β=1). Table 3 retains that ordering at higher shock extents. Large turnover harms all structures. The p.29 phrase “0.01%” conflicts with the parameter/table notation `p_turnover=.01` (one percent per period); this card follows the explicit parameter/table and flags the draft typo rather than claiming an optimum at 0.01 percent.

**Sensitivity and limit (Results §4.3, p.30; Appendix B, p.49).** Changing organization size from 280 to 140/210/350/420 and varying `p_learning` through .1–.6 leaves the broad inverted-U visible; at .7–.9 network-performance differences attenuate and average payoff falls. The appendix lists ranges for `m`, `z`, `s`, `β`, turnover and environmental shock extent. The 2008 draft's hierarchy variant is an unreported note and does not model authority or differential expertise. Rewiring provides a stylized network; sample/seed policy, executable code and final-publication revisions remain unverified.

**Transferable theory move (our analysis, untested).** The contribution is to turn structural separation into a *controlled knowledge-flow variable* and test its trade-off with access. Our recruitment feedback may be a broad common broadcast or differentiated candidate-specific guidance; this is an analogy, because applicants are not the paper's socially connected employees. A minimal new test should manipulate guidance reach/source differentiation while holding signal accuracy and candidate responsiveness fixed, record *both* accessibility to helpful guidance and concentration of skill investments, then test recovery after employer demand changes. A falsifier is an outcome difference with neither access nor diversity changing in the predicted directions. Majority-versus-direct-targeted advice should also be isolated: the Appendix C result warns that changing interpretation rules can nullify topology effects. The locked ABM has not run this experiment.
