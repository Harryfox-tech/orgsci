# Kocak, Levinthal and Puranam (2023) — Search and Coordination

**Theory move.** The paper crosses search difficulty with coordination difficulty and shows that hierarchical *belief influence* can help rapid attainment of good compatible actions when both challenges occur, even without superior leader knowledge, while crowd-like autonomy can prevail later. [Publisher HTML](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/orsc.2022.1601), *Organization Science* 34(2):851–869; [earlier card](../orgsci_core_reading_pack/05_kocak_levinthal_puranam_2023.md). **Coverage:** official HTML plus user-supplied 2022 publisher *Articles in Advance* PDF, 20 PDF pages, bearing an explicit not-final-copyedited notice; online appendix not supplied. See [source ledger](source_audit_first_five_2026_09_15.md).

1. **Phenomenon.** Individually promising choices can fail to combine under interdependent tasks [verified: task-environment framework].
2. **Existing theory / assumption.** Search-only settings vary payoff landscapes but can leave coordination trivial; pure-coordination settings can suppress search difficulty [verified: framework/Table 1].
3. **Why it matters.** A flat-versus-hierarchical comparison can change meaning across task environments. Expertise, authority and influence are also separable features of hierarchy [verified: discussion/endnote].
4. **Mechanism.** Adaptive individuals choose actions; payoff variability governs search difficulty and interdependence coordination difficulty; influence networks change belief similarity *and sharpness*. Individual and collective payoffs are aligned, abstracting from cooperation conflicts [Model §§3.1–3.3; advance PDF pp.7–10].
5. **Design / result.** Baseline `N=S=7`, `τ=.05`, 1,000 sampled task environments per influence structure, with `χ=0,.5,1` and cumulative performance evaluated at `T=50,100,500`. Flat teams dominate pure parallel search; hierarchy has early advantage with interdependence; crowds can dominate later [Results §4, Figure 2, advance PDF p.10].
6. **Contribution / limit.** Organizational form works differently when finding actions and making them jointly viable are both hard. The result concerns belief influence, not administrative hierarchy as a whole.
7. **Relation: contrast and possible boundary.** Candidates compete for limited job capacity, so one person's adaptation changes others' opportunity. This is **our inferred market-coordination analogy**, not an author claim. Before adding agents, test whether capacity coupling alone changes R2/R3 effects under varying role scarcity; specify candidate and employer payoff conflicts explicitly.

## Systematic theory-to-rule decomposition

**Puzzle and assumption gap, paper-verified.** Search models can ask whether actors find high-value actions while treating compatibility as incidental; coordination models can ask whether actors match while treating all matched actions as equal. The article constructs a task space in which *payoff variability* and *need for compatible actions* are independently adjustable, thereby exposing the joint problem. Its surprising premise is that hierarchy's benefit can arise from influence architecture, not a knowledgeable leader or misaligned incentives ([publisher, framework Table 1 and Model §§3.1–3.4](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/orsc.2022.1601)). Authors explicitly make individual and organizational payoffs aligned; the model cannot adjudicate political conflict.

**Minimal causal chain.** `Influence topology → similarity AND resolution of belief vectors → stability of choices and convergence on compatible actions → collective payoff`. Similarity alone is insufficient. Averaging beliefs can make them alike but diffuse, which increases switching; asymmetric ties around a leader preserve a stable, high-resolution focal belief and permit others to coordinate around it (publisher Results, Figure 3; Online Appendix §§3–4). This is a knowledge-*structure* effect analogous to Kane's open/closed sources, but the mediated state here is belief resolution as well as similarity.

**Formal anatomy, paper-verified (Model §§3.1–3.3).** There are `N` agents, each choosing one of `S` indexed discrete actions; baseline analyses use seven actors and `S=N` (publisher parameter table; verify exact experimental table before reproducing). One indexed action is randomly designated the global peak of value 1; remaining fixed baseline values are drawn from Uniform[`δ₀`,`δ₁`]. The coordination weight `χ` penalizes incompatible action profiles. At `χ=0`, an individual's contribution is independent of others; at `χ=1` and `δ₀=δ₁=1`, *which* compatible action is chosen no longer matters. Each agent has a belief vector `b_i` over actions, initialized as independently drawn values in [0,1]; only a chosen action's private assessment incorporates the payoff. Post-experience assessment is combined with the average belief of incoming influence neighbors at a self-weight `w` (publisher equations 1–3). Individual choice then depends on the belief vector and the action-selection rule. The payoff landscape is exogenous and static; belief and organizational action profiles are endogenous.

**Mechanism grounding.** The authors draw the payoff-average update from adaptive-learning work and the neighbor-average rule from DeGroot/social-influence theory (Model §3.2). The `χ` convergence bonus represents complementary compatibility, *not necessarily identical real-world work*. The information-source structure is the directed influence graph: unconnected crowds, reciprocally connected flat teams and hierarchical teams with asymmetric/acyclic leader ties. Saying merely “centralization helps” loses the causal property the Appendix isolates.

**Evidence chain and non-additivity.** In the dual-difficulty setting, hierarchy can reach good compatible configurations faster than a flat team or unconnected crowd, but fast convergence risks settling on a non-global peak when inferior options are costly (Results Figure 3 and discussion after it). Flat influence raises similarity yet lowers belief resolution, functionally increasing exploration; crowds may overtake flat teams in the long run because independent high-resolution payoff learning eventually coordinates behavior (Results around Figure 3). Lowering individual exploration improves flat teams relative to their baseline—an explicit structure × decision-policy interaction (Online Appendix §2). Two-agent tests show symmetric and asymmetric influence both increase similarity while degrading resolution, whereas asymmetry distributes resolution unequally (Appendix §3). Randomly dispersing the same number of asymmetric ties fails to reproduce the hierarchy result; giving the apex even one reciprocal subordinate tie sharply reduces performance (Appendix §4). Thus tie *acyclicity around the apex* is an isolated boundary, not “more hierarchy” as a scalar.

**Design audit, PDF-verified (Results §4 and Table 2, advance PDF p.10).** The paper varies `δ₀∈[0,1]`, `δ₁∈[δ₀,1]` on a .1 grid, and crosses `χ∈{0,.5,1}` with three influence structures. Baseline `N=S=7`, individual softmax temperature `τ=.05`, flat-team self-weight `w=.5`, crowd `w=1`, and apex `w=1` while subordinates retain flat-team influence. Authors average each structure over 1,000 sampled task environments and compare cumulative payoff at `T={50,100,500}` (Figure 2). The manuscript PDF does not state a modern paired-seed/random-stream policy; do not infer one. Results about rapid attainment of *satisfactory* choices must not be rewritten as always finding the global optimum.

## Primary-PDF mechanism and boundary audit

**Payoff and update order (Model §§3.1–3.3, advance PDF pp.7–9).** For action `k`, the fixed intrinsic value `v_k` is 1 for one randomly chosen global peak and otherwise sampled from Uniform[`δ₀`,`δ₁`]. An individual's payoff is `π_{i,k,t}=v_k[1−χ(1−F_{k,t})]`, where `F` is the fraction of *other* actors choosing compatible indexed action `k`. Thus `χ=0` removes coordination penalties without changing value heterogeneity; choosing the same index models compatible, not literally identical, work. Only the chosen action's private belief is updated toward experienced payoff using a running-average rule (Eq.2); a DeGroot-type weighted blend of this private assessment and incoming neighbors' assessments then forms the postinfluence belief vector (Eq.3). Softmax over that vector selects the next action (Eq.4). Influence acts on *beliefs*, not directly on action rights or rewards. The advance PDF does not provide code-level pseudorandom call order or a declaration of simultaneous versus sequential agent updating comparable to Kane Appendix B; do not assume it.

**Topology control (Model §3.3, Figure 1, PDF p.9).** All three archetypes are built by weights and ties: crowds have no social influence (`w=1`); flat teams have symmetric fully connected influence (`w=.5`); hierarchical teams retain the same follower network but give one apex actor `w=1`, so that other members receive its beliefs while it receives no neighbor beliefs. The apex has no initial expertise, payoff or task-feedback advantage. Its acyclic insulation, not formal permission to command, is the controlled change.

**Intermediate variables and why similarity alone fails (Analysis §5, Figure 3, PDF pp.10–13).** The mechanism panel sets `S=N=7, δ₀=0, δ₁=1, χ=1, τ=.05` over 500 periods, then measures six states: period payoff; action convergence by Herfindahl concentration; share of groups reaching the global peak; belief similarity as one minus mean pairwise absolute belief difference; belief resolution as within-vector maximum minus minimum; and period-to-period action instability. Flat teams become highly *similar* rapidly, but belief averaging makes their vectors diffuse/low-resolution and therefore their actions unstable. Crowds maintain sharper beliefs and eventually coordinate through task payoffs but lack top-down similarity. Hierarchy uniquely combines similarity and resolution because the stable apex broadcasts a sharp belief while followers still adjust; this yields fast convergence, but also premature entrapment on a good non-global action. The later crowd advantage is consequently a search-versus-coordination horizon reversal, not a blanket anti-hierarchy result.

**Isolations and interactions (Analysis §5, Structure §6, Robustness §7, PDF pp.13–15).** The main text reports online-appendix two-actor contrasts: both symmetric and asymmetric influence increase similarity and reduce resolution; asymmetry distributes resolution unequally. Randomly placing the *same six* asymmetric ties does not reproduce the apex result; letting one subordinate influence the apex disrupts acyclicity and sharply harms performance. Lower `τ` helps flat teams by compensating for structure-induced overexploration; greater follower autonomy (`w≈.9` in a fully connected flat team) improves long-run outcomes. A star with less mutual follower influence can outperform the full follower network in a high-χ setting. The paper further reports robustness to local rather than global task coupling, branching hierarchy/community graph scale, recency-sensitive payoff learning and differing `N/S` ratios; the appendix figures and exact statistics are **not independently inspected** here, so treat these as authors' summaries rather than appendix-verified cells.

**Boundary and contribution.** With `χ=0`, flat teams benefit from information sharing during pure parallel search; with interdependence and a near-term survival/selection horizon, a belief-stabilizing hierarchy helps coordination; with long time to find the global peak, crowds can overtake. When nonglobal values are low, hierarchy's early convergence can be costly. This changes the assumption that hierarchy's adaptation advantage must come from superior leaders, control rights or incentive correction. It does not show that bureaucratic hierarchy is generally good: real authority can bundle penalties, differential expertise and political conflicts absent here (Discussion §8, PDF pp.15–17). Any AI-recruitment analogy must separately defend *who influences whose beliefs* and whether candidate competition violates the aligned-payoff premise.

**Source status.** Main-text equations, baseline design, result contours and mechanism measures verified against the supplied advance PDF; online appendix and final copyedited numerical wording remain to audit. No new recruitment config, seed policy or computation result exists.

**Project handoff (our analysis, untested).** Employer capacity can couple candidate outcomes, but it is not the article's cooperative-action setting: candidates may compete and employer/candidate payoffs may diverge. First test a capacity-coupled variant *without* borrowing the influence network; then, only if the theory calls for it, compare common feedback broadcasts versus individually targeted feedback. Potential mediator: convergence of candidate investment beliefs and dispersion/resolution of their skill rankings. Falsifier: topology changes macro allocation without changing the predicted belief mediators. The article supports a general claim that interaction architecture can alter adaptation under joint search/coordination, not a direct claim about AI hiring or candidate rivalry.
