# Sharapov and Dahlander (2025) — Selection Regimes and Selection Errors

**Theory move.** The paper extends selection-process accounts beyond evaluator structure: stricter criteria can change both the submission pool and evaluators' lens, producing more errors despite a better average pool. [Full publisher HTML](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/orsc.2023.17482), [author institution's published PDF](https://spiral.imperial.ac.uk/entities/publication/8485e145-4ea5-4ff6-b3c3-91f64e524646), *Organization Science* 36(6):2324–2348. **Coverage:** full publisher main text; online appendix not audited.

1. **Phenomenon.** An accelerator changed three innovation-project selection regimes, seeking fewer false positives/negatives, yet mistakes persisted [verified: Abstract/Introduction].
2. **Existing theory / assumption.** Prior structure models analyze how selectors aggregate judgments over alternatives. This paper emphasizes that alternatives submitted and evaluation criteria may also change together [verified: Introduction/Theoretical Background]. It does **not** claim all earlier research holds pools exogenous.
3. **Why it matters.** Additional screening and applicant track record can appear to improve selection while eliciting different submissions and inducing misjudgment [verified: Introduction/Conclusion].
4. **Mechanism.** Evaluators placed greater weight on past entrepreneur success in a noisy setting; mean reversion weakened that signal. Within-type adverse selection let some experienced entrepreneurs submit marginal, lower-effort ideas while potentially promising newcomers were overlooked [verified: Abstract/Introduction/Discussion]. Entrepreneur submission and evaluator decision jointly produce later observed selection errors; this is an empirical process study, not an ABM.
5. **Design / non-obvious result.** The study combines 126 interviews and archival records of 3,580 eligible decisions, tracking rejected as well as funded applications. Three regimes evolved over time; the final stricter regime improved average submission quality yet evaluators became more error-prone. Authors examine pool composition, treatment through training/resources, learning and market evolution as alternatives [verified: Abstract/Data/Results]. These observational comparisons and mechanism triangulation do not equal randomized regime assignment.
6. **Contribution / limit.** The authors explicitly call for field experiments that disentangle evaluation criteria from selection structure and comparisons across organizations [verified: discussion before Conclusion]. Application ideas and entrepreneurial histories are not candidate skills or AI explanations.
7. **Relation: adopt selection-pool endogeneity, contrast mechanism.** Our central loop proposes that evaluation feedback changes candidates' **skill investment and verifiable evidence**, not merely application choice or idea effort. That extension must be tested by recording investment/evidence before next-round evaluation and isolating feedback from ranking; no AI recruitment effect follows from this study alone.

## Systematic selection-feedback audit

**Theory puzzle.** A stricter final selection process (`SR3`) appears to raise the average quality of ideas arriving at evaluation, yet does not simply lower selection errors. A regime is not only a selector applied to a fixed candidate set: it changes *who submits what*, which signals selectors privilege, and which error types become more likely ([publisher Abstract, Introduction and Discussion §§5–6](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/orsc.2023.17482)). This is the closest empirical analogue in the pack to our `evaluation_t → applicant adaptation → pool_{t+1}` loop, but the observable adaptation is entrepreneurial idea submission/effort rather than documented skill acquisition.

**Sequence and levels, paper-verified.** The organization (AppCampus) evolves through investment-board `SR1`, delegation `SR2`, and preselection `SR3`. Entrepreneurs choose whether and which mobile-app idea to submit; evaluators screen and fund; subsequent release/download evidence serves as a noisy ex post criterion for success. Interviews trace organizational reasoning for regime changes and app/team histories; archival records include rejected as well as funded applications. These are *observational longitudinal regimes*, not randomized assignments or agent-based simulations (publisher Data §3, Table 3, Results §4).

**Evidence denominator.** The paper reports 126 interviews and 3,580 eligible submission-level investment decisions after exclusions; Table 3 separates 1,774/1,137/669 submissions across SR1/SR2/SR3 and 143/100/184 funded decisions, respectively. False-positive percentages in Table 3 are denominated among *funded* projects, while a false negative concerns a rejected project that later succeeded. Outcome thresholds differ—unreleased, fewer than 10,000 downloads, 500,000+ or 1m+ downloads—and should not be collapsed into one timeless “accuracy” number. Logit models add application-category and evaluator fixed effects, but their regime coefficients are associations conditional on controls, not clean causal effects (publisher Tables 3–7 and model notes).

**Two opposed paths.** `More stringent solicitation/screening → higher mean observed submission quality` is one path. `Greater track-record emphasis × regression toward mean → newcomer false negatives` and `targeting experienced teams with outside funding options → marginal/low-commitment ideas from otherwise attractive teams → false positives` are two countervailing paths (publisher mechanism subsections after Table 7 and Discussion). The authors triangulate interview statements, rejection reasons and performance records; “mean reversion” and “within-type adverse selection” are *author interpretations supported by converging observations*, not experimentally isolated mediators. The final regime combines screening structure and changed criteria, so these effects cannot be attributed uniquely to one lever.

**Alternatives and boundary.** The paper considers composition of submitted ideas, support/training after selection, evaluator learning and market evolution as rival accounts (Introduction and Results/Discussion). Context is a single accelerator, small innovative app projects, funding stakes €20k–€70k, and ex post download outcomes. A marketplace where applicants cannot redeploy their best projects elsewhere would weaken the within-type adverse-selection path; a reliable track-record signal would weaken the mean-reversion problem. These are our theoretical boundary readings, not directly estimated counterfactual regimes. Authors explicitly request field experiments disentangling criteria from decision structure and cross-organization comparisons (Conclusion).

**Minimal recruitment test (our analysis, unrun).** Separate feedback *content* from ranking *threshold* at time `t`, then observe candidates' effort, skill investment, evidence production and next-period application before any `t+1` selection. Compare fixed-pool evaluation with endogenous-pool evaluation under common task environments; record pool composition and within-quality selection errors separately. A falsifier is that feedback affects the talent pool only through application timing, with no measurable investment/evidence adaptation. The source supports the importance of endogenous submission and criteria×pool interaction; it cannot prove an AI-specific candidate-skill response.

**Source status.** Publisher main text and tables verified; appendices and any causal decomposition of simultaneous regime changes remain not assessable. Our computational test has no config or results yet.
