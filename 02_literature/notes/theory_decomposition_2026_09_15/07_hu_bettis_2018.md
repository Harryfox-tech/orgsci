# Hu and Bettis (2018) — Multiple Organization Goals with Feedback

**Theory move.** The paper relaxes the single high-level goal in performance-feedback theory and examines how shared task environments make feedback on multiple operational goals interdependent and hard to attribute. [Publisher record](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/orsc.2018.1207), *Organization Science* 29(5):873–889. **Coverage:** publisher record plus [author-uploaded forthcoming full text](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/323401414_Multiple_Organization_Goals_with_Feedback_from_Shared_Technological_Task_Environments); online appendices and final-version changes not audited.

1. **Phenomenon.** Organizations use goal feedback to adapt, yet operational goals can share technological tasks [verified: abstract].
2. **Existing theory / assumption.** Much feedback research examines one overall goal and misses lower-level operational interdependence [authors' characterization in abstract].
3. **Why it matters.** A change in one goal may contain information about other shared tasks, confounding learning and credit assignment [verified: abstract; exact causal structure pending].
4. **Mechanism / method.** Feedback on safety, fuel efficiency and reliability with shared technological environments is analyzed by panel vector autoregression in the automobile industry [author full text, Method]. This is **not a simulation**; agents, state-update equations and ABM seed policy are inapplicable.
5. **Result.** Interdependent feedback can create severe, misleading confusion in learning from goals [publisher abstract, author text Results]. Numeric signs require specific table/IRF qualifications, not a generic “all goals conflict” summary.
6. **Contribution / limit.** Operational task coupling qualifies single-goal feedback reasoning. The paper supplies no direct evidence on applicant responses.
7. **Relation: contrast.** Our outcomes—conditional fit, placement, learning, evidence conversion and resource gap—need separate definitions. **Analysis:** a common feedback signal could improve one measure while worsening another. Avoid treating an aggregate hiring score as proof of all goals; testing cross-goal credit assignment would require a new, specified mechanism.

## Systematic multi-goal mechanism audit

**Theoretical assumption gap.** A single overall aspiration can hide the lower operational goals and shared technological means by which it is pursued. The paper explicitly contrasts the traditional focus on overall performance with a March–Simon goal hierarchy, then defines feedback interdependency: an action aimed at one operational goal can unintentionally alter feedback on another goal because both share an engineering task environment (author version Introduction, around pp.1–3). The substantive problem is not merely multiple outcome labels; it is that feedback on each label is no longer causally separable.

**Causal loop and identification distinction.** `Design/action for goal A → shared technical task → observed A and B outcomes → inference about which action worked → next design/action`. The action-to-outcome mapping is organizationally complex. The PVAR estimates *cross-goal performance dynamics*, not engineers' actual psychological belief updates. Thus “credit assignment becomes harder” is an author-theoretical inference from interdependent feedback, not an observed micro-level mediator. An ABM that directly programs confused agents would be a separate model assumption (author Introduction, Methods, Discussion).

**Empirical anatomy, author-version verified.** The unit is an automobile *model-year* in the U.S. light-vehicle market, with models active during 1980–2009; performance data are collected primarily from Consumer Reports. Measures concern safety, fuel efficiency and reliability at the vehicle-model level. The descriptive/correlation table reports 2,583 observations for its joint variable set; individual-variable Ns differ, so this cannot be casually called “2,583 firms.” The panel VAR uses lagged values of all three goals, with model and year effects; the authors estimate a one-year-lag system GMM representation and inspect impulse-response functions over six subsequent periods (author text Methods, Tables 1–3, Figure 2).

**Methodological evidence chain.** PVAR is suited to reciprocal and lagged goal relationships, whereas three separate single-outcome regressions would miss feedback across goals. Table 3 reports nine one-year-lag coefficients; Figure 2 shows a one-unit shock's time profile and 90% intervals, generated with 500 repetitions according to the figure discussion. Cholesky identification depends on variable ordering; authors examine all orderings and report consistent qualitative conclusions, although the alternative charts are available from authors rather than in this card. Neither a VAR impulse response nor an engineering-design explanation uniquely identifies a micro-level causal action; model-year and year controls remove some heterogeneity but not all unmeasured time-varying design choices (author Methods/Results, Figure 2 text).

**Mechanism heterogeneity and non-obvious result.** The article distinguishes congruent, divergent, unidirectional and asymmetric cross-goal relationships rather than assuming all trade-offs are negative. Reliability–efficiency feedback is discussed as directionally asymmetric in this sample (author Results and Figure 4). An improvement in one technological outcome may have a delayed benefit, cost or one-way effect on another. The apparent same-time or lagged cross-goal correlations are *aggregate outcomes of many engineering decisions*, not one clean design rule. This matters for our proposed AI evaluations because a single score may conceal divergent effects on placement, candidate learning and access.

**Combinatorial boundary.** With three goals, the authors count nine possible bilateral relationship types for each of three pairs, hence 9³=729 relationship combinations; with four, 9⁶=531,441. This is an *upper-bound combinatorial illustration under simplifying assumptions*, not the number observed in the automobile sample (author theory section around p.20). It motivates careful goal selection and modularity tests, not an instruction to add many ABM outcomes.

**Project test (our analysis, unrun).** Specify two recruitment goals that share a candidate resource constraint, e.g. fit quality and access equity. Introduce an evaluation-feedback change aimed only at fit and observe candidate investment and both outcomes. A mechanism variable should record which evidence signals are used and whether a response targeted to one goal changes the other through the same skill-investment pathway. Falsifier: when the goals' task demands are made independent, the predicted cross-goal response persists unchanged. The paper supports multi-goal feedback interdependency as a theory warning; it does not establish any specific AI recruitment sign or effect size.

**Source status.** Author-uploaded full text supports design and qualitative findings; published final wording/online appendices and causal micro-behavior are not fully verified.
