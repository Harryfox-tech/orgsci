# Brahm and Poblete (2024) — Organizational Culture, Adaptation, and Performance

**Theory move.** A cultural-evolution formal model distinguishes belief accuracy from prosocial values and shows that they affect innovation, tradition, diffusion and adaptation through different choices. [Publisher record](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/orsc.2022.16791), [author preprint PDF](https://lbsresearch.london.edu/id/eprint/3654/1/culture%20and%20adaptation%20-%20with%20authors%20-%2020241101%20-%20FINAL.pdf), *Organization Science* 35(5):1823–1848. **Coverage:** author preprint main text, 63 PDF pages, inspected; publisher abstract; supplementary derivations and final-version changes not fully audited.

1. **Phenomenon.** Tradition and innovation compete under uncertain environmental change [verified: abstract].
2. **Existing theory / assumption.** The authors revisit a view of culture as hindering adaptation and consider how culture can enable it [verified: abstract].
3. **Why it matters.** Beliefs about the environment and willingness to help others are different cultural channels; a single culture scalar could mask opposing effects [first clause verified; latter our analysis].
4. **Mechanism.** Members choose costly individual learning/innovation or cheaper but potentially obsolete social learning/tradition after an environmental signal. They can exert costly helping effort to facilitate others' social learning; leadership-controlled belief accuracy and prosocial values change interpretation and help [author preprint §§3–4]. This is a formal cultural-evolution model, not a stochastic ABM run archive.
5. **Conditional result.** More accurate beliefs improve *timing* of innovation and use/diffusion of tradition, mitigating their trade-off; stronger prosocial values reduce the cost of tradition but reduce adaptation, leading to inverted-U performance in their modeled setting [author preprint Propositions 1–2, pp.34–37]. Numerical illustrations are not empirical effect estimates.
6. **Contribution / limit.** Culture's components influence boundedly rational adaptation differently; this is a formal-model implication, not a hiring-policy estimate.
7. **Relation: boundary/contrast.** Candidate interpretation accuracy and social support for evidence creation could plausibly differ. Such variables are **new hypotheses**, not licensed additions to the locked ABM. First establish that a candidate-support institution changes a named theoretical assumption and yields a falsifiable interaction.

## Systematic cultural-mechanism reconstruction

**Puzzle, not a “culture good/bad” contest.** Conventional arguments can frame a strong tradition as inertia under change. The authors separate two cultural dimensions that operate through different micro choices: a shared *causal belief* about interpreting an environmental signal, and a norm promoting costly help to others. The non-obvious claim is that better beliefs can make both costly innovation and cheap social learning work *at the right times*, while too much prosocial help can make tradition efficient enough to crowd out necessary adaptation (author preprint Introduction, Theory §2 and Propositions 1–2).

**Formal anatomy, preprint verified (§§3.1–3.3).** A large organization is represented as a continuum of members of measure `N`, rather than an executable population of finite agents with a seed. The world has changing states, with per-period change probability `p`; the correct “technology” is state-specific and earns normalized benefit 1, an obsolete one 0. Individual learning discovers the current technology at cost `C`; social learning copies a member's technology at lower cost `c` but can fail after a state change. Members observe a signal whose distribution depends on change and use a cutoff for selecting individual versus social learning. They do not observe the truth or their culture's belief accuracy. Helping effort `a_i` can lower a follower's social-learning cost but imposes an increasing convex personal cost `m(a_i)` on the helper, producing a social dilemma (preprint pp.16–25). The stock of tuned knowledge and frequency of learning modes are endogenous; environmental change, cost assumptions and shared cultural dimensions are imposed.

**Named assumptions and grounding.** The model assumes *everyone shares the same causal beliefs*—a maximally strong culture—while their accuracy/verisimilitude varies. Belief accuracy is a stable organization-level dimension; prosocial intensity is separately set by leadership. These are not heterogeneous individual belief vectors or a time-updated culture in the baseline. The authors link selective individual/social learning to cultural-evolution theory and helping to organizational knowledge-transmission dilemmas (preprint Theory §2, Model §§3.1–3.3). Consequently, a “belief accuracy” result must not be misread as a result about personalized AI guidance.

**Decision and feedback loop.** Environmental state → noisy signal filtered through common causal beliefs → each member's learning cutoff → individual innovation or copying of tuned/obsolete practice → optional help and personal payoff → social transmission and evolutionary selection of cutoffs/help traits. In §4.1, a small fraction copies the learning threshold and help trait of higher-payoff members via replicator dynamics; this is *strategy imitation distinct from social learning of technology*. The paper distinguishes individual expected payoff and total organizational fitness, so helping can be privately costly yet collectively valuable. No ABM update-order artifact, paired-seed policy or 30-run simulation claim applies to this analytical formal model.

**Mediator chain for Proposition 1 (preprint Figures 2–4, p.34).** More accurate shared beliefs improve detection of *when* environmental change occurred. Innovation becomes more timely; afterward social learning is used more during stable periods, diffusing the successful technology at less cost. Greater use of copying increases the marginal value of help. The authors explicitly qualify that the *overall frequency* of innovation may fall as its timing/productivity improves, and the total innovation–tradition trade-off does not literally disappear. A summary saying “accuracy raises innovation and tradition” therefore needs this timing-versus-frequency qualification.

**Opposing paths for Proposition 2 (preprint pp.35–37).** Prosocial values induce more helping and lower the effective cost of tradition: positive efficiency channel. They also reinforce social learning rather than costly individual discovery when the world changes: negative adaptation channel. Their relative weights yield an inverted-U modeled relationship between prosocial intensity and performance. This is a theory implication with numerical model illustrations, not a randomized field estimate or a universal optimum level.

**Boundary and missing tests.** State-change probability, signal informativeness, individual/social costs and transmission help all affect the relative merit of the two strategies. The baseline has a stable, uniformly shared causal belief, so it does not assess disagreement, learning about belief accuracy, network isolation or applicant heterogeneity. Exact calibration, proof conditions and appendix robustness must be checked before reproducing a parameter claim. A direct analogy to candidate feedback should contrast common inaccurate guidance against differentiated accurate guidance, while holding social support separate; the locked baseline must remain untouched.

**Project handoff (our analysis, untested).** A minimal theoretical extension would compare feedback interpretation accuracy with support for generating verifiable evidence, using candidate investment timing, skill renewal after changed demand and evidence diffusion as distinct mediators. Predicted non-additivity: support may help candidates complete current evidence but crowd out exploration under turbulent demand. Falsifier: varying support changes outcomes without altering social-learning/evidence costs, or changing guidance accuracy fails to improve the timing of skill renewal. The paper supports separation of cultural belief and help channels, not an empirical claim about AI hiring.

**Source status.** The author-hosted main text and publisher abstract support architecture and propositions. Published-version differences, exact proofs/supplement and any recruitment result remain unverified.
