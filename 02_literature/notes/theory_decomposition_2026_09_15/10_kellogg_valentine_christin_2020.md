# Kellogg, Valentine and Christin (2020) — Algorithms at Work

**Theory move.** The review extends organizational-control theory by specifying six ways algorithms direct, evaluate and discipline workers, while documenting worker agency and resistance. [Author full PDF](https://mvalentine.github.io/pdfs/algorithms-at-work.pdf), *Academy of Management Annals* 14(1):366–410, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.5465/annals.2018.0174). **Coverage:** full author PDF, chiefly pp. 366–368, 372–373, 390–395 inspected; remaining cases and references not systematically audited. Adjacent management review, not *Organization Science* or a simulation.

1. **Phenomenon.** Algorithms reshape managerial direction, evaluation and discipline; workers can contest those systems [verified: abstract, pp. 366–368].
2. **Existing theory / assumption.** The authors use Edwards' contested-terrain lens and contrast algorithmic with technical and bureaucratic control [verified: Introduction and framework, pp. 367–373].
3. **Why it matters.** An algorithm is not a unitary treatment: differing control mechanisms invite differing responses, and agency remains even where measures appear comprehensive [verified: framework/conclusion].
4. **Mechanism.** The six Rs are **restricting and recommending** (direction), **recording and rating** (evaluation), **replacing and rewarding** (discipline) [verified: abstract and p. 372]. Recommending offers suggestions intended to prompt targeted decisions (p. 372); the review also describes practical resistance, including ignoring or gaming recommendations/scores (p. 391). This is synthesis of prior work, not a formal agent update rule.
5. **Evidence / non-obvious implication.** The paper organizes interdisciplinary evidence and develops `algoactivism` as individual and collective responses (pp. 390–395). It does not estimate one pooled causal effect or compare ABM regimes, so experimental unit, baselines, robustness and simulation outcomes are inapplicable. A theoretical implication is that more detailed algorithmic control need not erase worker agency.
6. **Contribution / limit.** Control and resistance are jointly relevant to algorithmic work. Its objects are workers in organizations; applicants before employment require a carefully argued extension.
7. **Relation: adopt recommending/rating distinction, contrast population.** Our R1 evaluation and R2 guidance are distinct affordances; candidate uptake or gaming is **our hypothesis**, not this review's recruitment finding. Audit whether explanations alter investment or only observable evidence, and test neutral versus targeted guidance before calling R2 a causal feedback mechanism.

## Systematic control-and-response decomposition

**Real theoretical question.** When software partly replaces managerial direction and assessment, what forms of control arise, and how do workers retain agency? The authors do not compare “algorithm/no algorithm” as an accuracy treatment. They place algorithms on Edwards' contested terrain of managerial control and worker response, extending technical and bureaucratic control arguments to data-intensive direction, evaluation and discipline ([author PDF, Introduction/framework pp.367–373](https://mvalentine.github.io/pdfs/algorithms-at-work.pdf)).

**Mechanism architecture (paper-verified, Table/framework around p.372).** Direction consists of *restricting* available actions and *recommending* preferred ones. Evaluation consists of *recording* activity and *rating* it. Discipline consists of *replacing* workers or opportunities and *rewarding* measured behavior. These are six analytically separate affordances, grouped into three control functions. A recommendation can change future choices without barring others; a rating can alter incentives without teaching the rated person what to do; a restriction can change the feasible set even if beliefs remain intact. They are not mutually exclusive in one platform. Calling all six “AI feedback” would erase their different information and resource pathways.

**Process and intermediate states (our analytic formalization).** `Algorithmic classification/measurement → feasible set, suggested action or score → worker interpretation and response → observed task behavior → next measure/recommendation`. Only the six-R typology and worker-response evidence are author claims; this loop is our translation for model comparison. Possible mediators differ: access to options for restricting, belief/choice targeting for recommending, visibility for recording, perceived rank for rating, labor/opportunity availability for replacing, and payoff/incentive salience for rewarding. The review itself does not define finite agents, payoff equations, update order or a simulated `t+1` array.

**Countervailing channel: algoactivism.** Workers can comply, evade, game, negotiate or collectively contest algorithmic prescriptions. The authors use *algoactivism* for forms of resistance and response rather than assuming comprehensive metrics imply comprehensive control (author PDF pp.390–395). This is a crucial organizational-theory boundary for any candidate model that currently hard-codes automatic uptake of AI feedback. Nevertheless, the paper synthesizes workplace cases; whether job applicants have comparable information, bargaining power or collective capacity is not established by the review.

**Evidence type and limits.** This is an interdisciplinary conceptual review; its evidence is prior empirical studies and conceptual classification, not one identified causal design. A “main result,” replication unit, seed policy, sensitivity analysis and short/long ranking reversal cannot be supplied in Kane-style simulation terms. The correct systematic reading is to identify its theoretical claims, which mechanisms are evidenced by cited workplace studies, and where our model would need a test. A source pointer to this review supports a typology or a possible organizational response, not a numerical R1/R2 treatment effect in hiring.

**Project contrast and isolation (our analysis, untested).** R1 ranking aligns most directly with *rating* and possibly *restricting* at a shortlist threshold; R2 individualized advice aligns with *recommending*; R3 resource gaps may resemble *rewarding/replacing* if access or incentives change. These are conceptual mappings, not authors' model labels. A minimal isolation test must vary ranking visibility, guidance content and feasible option set separately, measure uptake and strategic evidence manipulation, and distinguish genuine skill investment from score-facing presentation. If advice changes measured evidence but not skill stocks, the “candidate-learning feedback” interpretation is falsified or at least narrowed. If a rating is hidden from candidates, an alleged candidate-adaptation channel cannot operate without another information path.

**Source status.** Full author-hosted PDF is available; six-R framework and algoactivism discussion verified. Individual case-by-case study quality and recruitment-specific resistance remain to be audited before citation as evidence.
